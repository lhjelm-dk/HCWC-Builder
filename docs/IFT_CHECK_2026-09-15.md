# Oil–water interfacial tension: the literature check behind `PLAN_DUAL_PHASE_SEAL.md` §4

**15 September 2026.** Step 0 of the two-phase seal plan asked whether the app's oil–water
interfacial-tension line is being used outside its range. It is, and its provenance is doubtful.
Nothing in the code is changed by this note; §5 sets out the choice.

## 1 · What the app computes

`hcwc/core/seals.py` carries two correlations attributed to Aplin & Yang (1998):

| | dyne/cm (= mN/m) | 30 °C | 50 °C | 70 °C | 90 °C | 110 °C | 130 °C |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| gas–water | `91.657·exp(−0.0126 T)` | 62.8 | 48.8 | 37.9 | 29.5 | 22.9 | 17.8 |
| oil–water | `−0.1886 T + 24.866` | 19.2 | 15.4 | 11.7 | 7.9 | 4.1 | 0.3 |
| ratio | | 0.31 | 0.32 | 0.31 | 0.27 | 0.18 | 0.02 |

`docs/SEAL_CAPACITY_REVIEW.md` already records that both were "reproduced as given and have not
been traced to their source". Yang & Aplin (1998), *Marine and Petroleum Geology* 15, is a paper on
mudstone pore-size distribution and modelled permeability; it is the source of the pore-throat
relation and does not contain interfacial-tension correlations. Where the two lines came from is
not known.

## 2 · What the literature says about oil–water tension at reservoir conditions

Dead crude oil against brine at ambient conditions: Buckley & Fan (2005, SCA2005-01; 2007,
*Petrophysics* 48) measure about 20–35 mN/m across their crude-oil set and cite Donaldson et al.'s
43 crudes at 9.9–31.5 mN/m against 0.1 M NaCl.

A 796-point compilation of crude-oil/brine measurements over 25–140 °C and 1–314 bar (Scientific
Reports 2024, 14:28470) spans 1.6–64.6 mN/m with a median of 23.4 mN/m; temperature is the
strongest single sensitivity, and the bulk of the data sit between 15 and 30 mN/m.

Temperature does not drive live-oil tension down steeply. Hjelmeland & Larrondo (1986, *SPE
Reservoir Engineering* 1, 321–328), on North Sea crudes recombined to bubble point, found that
under anaerobic conditions crude-oil/brine tension *increased* with temperature, decreasing only
under aerobic conditions. Sauerer et al. (2017, SPE-183671) report "a common trend of IFT increase
from the dead oil at ambient to the live oil at reservoir conditions".

Seal-capacity practice: Schowalter (1979) took 21 dyne/cm as the best value for 30–40 °API oils
and 30 dyne/cm for subsurface gas–water; the AAPG Wiki's seal pages give 15–72 dyne/cm overall;
ZetaWare's seal calculator states oil–water 10–40 and gas–water 30–60 dyne/cm, "with higher IFT at
low pressure (shallow depth) and dry gas", and that in-situ values are usually lower than published
numbers. Firoozabadi & Ramey (1988, *JCPT* 27) correlate hydrocarbon–water tension on density
difference and reduced temperature and report measurements on three reservoir oil–brine systems;
the full text is paywalled.

The gas line is consistent with this: 38–49 mN/m at 50–70 °C is where methane–brine sits at
reservoir pressure.

The oil line is not. At 70 °C it gives 11.7 mN/m, at 90 °C 7.9 and at 110 °C 4.1, against a
measured envelope of roughly 15–30 mN/m across the whole 25–140 °C range and a slope that the
live-oil measurements put near zero or positive. Below about 60 °C the line is within the low end
of the data; above it, it is outside every source found.

## 3 · What this does to the shipped seal capacity

The app's default top-seal inputs (56–87 °C from the 2 050 m burial, θ 0–30°, seal throats
0.01–0.10 µm, reservoir throats 2.0–3.5 µm, ρ_w 1.00–1.10, ρ_oil 0.70–0.85), 20 000 draws:

| oil–water tension | seal capacity P90 / P50 / P10, m of column |
|---|---:|
| the app's line (P50 11 mN/m over the temperature range) | 79 / 148 / 441 |
| 21 mN/m, Schowalter | 151 / 274 / 821 |
| 25 mN/m | 180 / 326 / 977 |

The oil capacity is understated by a factor of about 1.9 at the default temperature. A second
symptom: the same seal inputs with gas (ρ 0.20–0.35) give P50 167 m, so the app currently holds a
*shorter* oil column than gas column, where the standard result (Schowalter's own worked case) is
an oil column roughly twice the gas column for the same seal.

The top seal controls about a third of the default prospect's realisations, so this reaches the
headline contact distribution and the calibration against the NCS record on tab 6.

## 4 · What it does to the two-phase question

The gas share in `PLAN_DUAL_PHASE_SEAL.md` §3 depends on σ_o/σ_g. With the app's line the ratio
is 0.31 and the share 45 %. With oil at 21–25 mN/m against the gas line's 37.9 at 70 °C the ratio
is 0.55–0.66, and the share falls to about 16–24 %, bracketing Hood's 20 %. The disagreement with
Hood was the oil tension line and nothing else: with a sourced oil tension the derivation
reproduces his number from the inputs, with no rule-of-thumb toggle.

## 5 · The choice

Reading 2 of §4 is confirmed, with the addition that the line's provenance is unknown. Three ways
forward, for Lars:

1. **Replace the oil line with an elicited range, temperature-flat.** Default 18–28 mN/m (median
   23, the compilation's median and Schowalter's 21 inside it), shown as a slider beside the
   temperature with its sources in the help. The gas line stays. Shipped oil seal capacities rise
   by about 1.9×; the example prospects, the paper's worked numbers and the NCS comparison move.
2. **Keep a temperature dependence but bound it.** A weak negative slope from the compilation
   (about −0.05 mN/m per °C from 25 mN/m at 25 °C) with a floor at 15 mN/m. Closer in form to
   what is there; the slope is not well supported by the live-oil studies.
3. **Leave the line and state the limitation.** Not recommended: the capacity it produces is
   outside every source found at the temperatures the tool is used at, and the tool's posture is
   to compute from inputs it can defend.

The recommendation is 1. It is the smallest change that makes the input defensible, it puts the
number where an assessor can see and argue with it, and it settles §4 of the two-phase plan
without a toggle.
